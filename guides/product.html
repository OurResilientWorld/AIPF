Agile Product Guide

Help Agile teams manage growing AI capacity, test small changes and decide what to build, improve and support.

By Rick Molony and Ghulam Akbar · developed with AI assistance

1 Part 1 — Understand the Approach

1.1 Manage what AI makes possible#

AI rapidly increases the amount of work a team can generate. Human capacity to judge usefulness, check evidence, integrate changes and support the result does not grow at the same rate. Producing more work without managing those limits creates queues, rework and products that are difficult to use or maintain.

The Agile Product Guide helps Product Owners, product managers and Agile teams manage that growing capacity. Use it within your existing backlog, short delivery cycles, user feedback and operating practices. Choose what is worth doing, test a small connected part and decide how much more work the evidence justifies.

A product may be software, a service, report, policy, learning resource or internal workflow. This guide applies when AI helps create or run the work, even when the finished product contains no AI. Product decisions remain with people; technical delivery and review belong with those able to judge them.

The Product Owners AI Guide supports a Product Owner who needs an accessible route from an idea through a simulation and steel thread to an investment decision. This guide adapts the same purpose for people working with Agile product delivery. Both approaches are useful for their intended audiences.

Use the Framework Purpose for the shared rationale and the Framework Glossary for unfamiliar terms.

1.2 Keep the product and the next bet clear#

Name who the product serves, the outcome you want, what it owns, its dependencies and what is outside its scope. A bet commits limited time, money or effort to answer a question. Give it an owner, an effort limit and a condition that would stop the work. Do not create a separate application or team for every capability; revisit the boundary as you learn.

Invented example: a service team wants customers to receive correct answers without repeating their requests. Its next bet asks whether an AI summary preserves everything staff need. It tests a small part of the request-to-response journey before committing to wider use.

1.3 Five questions for the next piece of work#

  1. Who needs help, and what would improve for them?
  2. What are we assuming, and what could show us we are wrong?
  3. What is the smallest useful next step?
  4. Does the result meet its purpose, including when something goes wrong?
  5. Who can accept it, support it or stop it?

Ask these questions for a new product or an important change. Match checks to the consequences of failure; explain why lighter checks are enough for a small, reversible edit. Start with How to Use the Guide, then use Principles & Responsibilities when assigning work and review.

Use these phases to direct growing AI-enabled capacity towards work that people can judge and use. Start where your evidence places you. Keep the purpose, next question and decision visible; open the working details when you need them.

2.1 Phase 1 — Validate, Show, and Set Boundaries#

Aim: understand the need and show the intended experience before using AI to produce more work. Keep: a clear product purpose, important assumptions, a concept, credible options and limits for the next test.

Gate 1: the authorised product decision-maker decides whether to proceed with a limited test, revise, defer or stop. Record the owner, allowed scope, limits and next review.

Working details — understand the need and prepare the test
  • Check the need. Talk with or observe affected people. Compare a new build with a smaller change, an existing service or doing nothing. Define correct and unacceptable results. Label invented examples and AI-generated personas as assumptions, not research findings.
  • Show the whole experience and the small step. Use personas and a journey map to describe needs, barriers and handoffs. Mark what is included now, deferred, excluded or undecided. A story map can place the next small item beneath the whole journey. Use the concept to clarify intent; keep it easy to change or discard.
  • Choose evidence that could change the plan. Record important assumptions, their sources and dates, uncertainty, owners and contrary findings. Name a result that would defeat the idea—a falsifier—and choose the cheapest useful test that could find it.
  • Compare options and agree capacity. For each credible option, show the next question, dependencies, estimated effort and cost, checking/support needs and review condition. Keep estimates, approved spending and actual costs distinct. A preferred roadmap is not an approved budget.
  • Set the AI rules. Agree permitted information and actions, success checks, review points and who runs them. Identify what must stop or return to the owner. Share relevant dependencies with family or portfolio owners using the same evidence references.

2.2 Phase 2 — Prove the End-to-End Evidence Slice#

Aim: test one small connected part against the question that matters. Keep: the tested version, cases, comparison, results, failures, recovery, actual costs and remaining uncertainty. “Prove” means collect evidence for that question; it does not promise certainty.

Gate 2: proceed with the next justified investment, revise, defer or stop. An MVP is usually built after a Gate 2 Proceed and judged at Gate 3. Funding the next step is distinct from accepting or releasing it.

Working details — test the connected slice
  • Make the test inspectable. Record the question, tested version, comparison, cases, pass/fail criteria, method, results, limits and reviewer. Use real or representative conditions, including unusual cases and recovery. Identify simulated parts so nobody mistakes them for working behaviour.
  • Check beyond the maker’s account. Use original sources or separately established examples. Implementation and tests can share the same mistake; agreement between AI tools alone is not an independent check. Resolve material technical defects and rerun affected checks.
  • Make controls work during development. Pause dependent work after a failed check or missing important evidence. Correct errors within scope; take unresolved purpose, scope, assumptions or material-risk choices to the owner with options and a recommendation.
  • Judge the learning and cost. Compare actual effort, review burden and remaining uncertainty with the proposed next step. The evidence supports only the claims tested. A demonstration or one successful test does not establish user value or complete coverage.

Invented example: take a request through AI summary creation and staff checking. Include a request with an important exception and one the AI cannot usefully summarise. Check whether staff can recover from the original request.

2.3 Phase 3 — Decide and Operationalise#

Aim: learn from use and decide what is fit for continued operation. Keep: user and operating results, benefits and burdens, the accepted scope, support owners and triggers for review. “Operationalise” means put the people and support in place to keep using the result.

Gate 3: proceed, revise, defer, stop, or scale when evidence and operating support justify wider use. Record the intended use, evidence, limits, authorised accepter, support responsibilities and next review.

Working details — acceptance and ongoing use
  • Inspect what users experience. Compare observed outcomes with the Product Intent. Include accessibility, quality, rights, disclosure, security, ongoing costs, user burden and support needs. An attractive demonstration does not establish these results.
  • Prepare acceptance. Give the owner a demonstration and a plain-English account of checks, unresolved issues and support arrangements, with evidence links. Resolve known defects that could materially affect the intended use and explain remaining limits. No process guarantees a product has no bugs.
  • Make each permission explicit. Acceptance, funding, release, wider reuse and scale are distinct decisions, even when they share evidence. Name who holds each authority. The maker cannot be the sole accepter.
  • Keep learning after acceptance. Monitor results, incidents, cost and changes to AI behaviour or supporting information. Reopen affected decisions when their basis changes. Record what changed, who acts and who else must recheck earlier work.

AI Product Acceptance, also called “Done, Done”, means that an authorised person accepts a specific result for a stated use, with evidence, limitations and support recorded. “Generated”, “reviewed” and “passes a test” describe progress; none alone means accepted.

2.4 Use a gate to decide, not to keep work moving automatically#

A gate is a decision by someone with authority. Use the options below to control how much work starts and what may happen next. Stop conditions can interrupt work at any time. Existing delegated decisions stay with their owners; sharing evidence does not create a second approval requirement.

Decision What happens next Available
Proceed Take the next justified step within agreed scope and limits. Every gate
Revise Change the idea, approach or scope and recheck affected evidence. Every gate
Defer Wait for a named condition and arrange to check it. Every gate
Stop End the current commitment and handle remaining obligations. Every gate
Scale Widen use when real-use evidence and operating support justify it. Gate 3 only

Turn the decision into action. Update the backlog, owner, limits and review point. Finish or stop existing work before adding more than the team can judge and support. Use the Framework Practices for the shared learning loop.

3 Part 3 — Principles & Responsibilities

In this tab

  • Apply the principles to the next backlog decision

Use these responsibilities to manage increased AI-enabled capacity without overloading review, integration or support. The Framework Principles explain the shared foundations; this tab shows their application in Agile product work.

3.1 Apply the principles to the next backlog decision#

Choose the need before the output. Use observed needs to order work. Consider a smaller change or no new build before increasing production.

Test what could change your mind. Keep observations, estimates and AI suggestions distinct. Check original sources and contrary findings.

Limit work to review and support capacity. Finish, narrow or stop active work before adding more. Past spending is not a reason to continue.

Name who accepts and supports use. Judge benefit alongside quality, risk, rights, recovery and ongoing cost. Include a real independent challenge.

Let learning change the backlog. Act on findings and revisit decisions when users, sources, models or costs change. Recording lessons alone is not enough.

These apply the Framework’s Judgement, Evidence, Restraint, Accountability and Adaptation principles. Use the shared explanation when you need the wider reasoning.

3.2 Make each responsibility someone’s job#

Keep these six responsibilities clear without creating six new posts. Name the product decision-maker—a Product Owner, product manager, founder or other authorised person—and specialists where judgement needs their expertise. If evidence is insufficient, seek help, narrow the intended use or stop.

Responsibility What the owner makes sure happens
Product Purpose, priorities, scope, trade-offs and gate decisions have a named owner.
Customer and User Evidence Real needs and results are checked; invented material is labelled.
AI Model Harness Checks run, problems trigger action and rules remain suitable as work changes.
Delivery Design, information, security, building, testing and release are handled properly; defects are corrected and checks rerun.
Operations Monitoring, support, maintenance and recovery have owners and a known cost.
Independent Review Assumptions and results are challenged during work and before acceptance using evidence beyond the maker’s account.

One person may hold several responsibilities. Reviewers still need the time, competence and authority to challenge the work. A reviewer AI can help; its agreement does not replace independent evidence or the authorised person’s decision.

3.3 Keep AI use within clear rules#

The AI Model Harness is the working set of rules and checks around AI; it need not be a new platform. Run validation during the work so the Product Owner need not supervise every action. Keep purpose, investment, material-risk and gate decisions with accountable people.

Practical controls — agree, check, correct and escalate
Check What to make explicit
Intent What AI may do, for whom and within which limits.
Evidence Approved information and sources supporting the output.
Evaluation Important cases, omissions, mistakes and success criteria.
Safety What must be prevented, including disclosure to the wrong person.
Observability How problems are recorded, detected and brought to someone who can act.
Change What must be rechecked after a model, prompt, instruction, tool, source or access change.
  1. Agree checks, limits, review points and who runs them before starting. AI or automated tools can run routine checks; independent review and acceptance still need capable, authorised people.
  2. Compare results and assumptions with the intent and sources before later work relies on them. Pause dependent work when a check fails or important evidence is missing.
  3. Correct errors within scope and rerun affected checks. Bring unresolved purpose, scope, assumptions or material-risk choices to the owner with options and a recommendation; record the decision before affected work resumes.
  4. Test the harness with a known error or unsupported assumption in a safe test. Check that it detects the problem and stops dependent work. Repair missed checks; one successful test does not establish complete coverage.

Skills, plugins and agents do not themselves grant permission. Link shared dependencies and permissions to the family and portfolio records. Name who can narrow, stop or restore use; changed tools must not quietly inherit acceptance for a different version.

More automatic action requires evidence and suitable controls. Automatic acceptance means pre-authorised routine checks within a named boundary, with checks able to detect the producing system’s mistakes and sampled human inspection. Record the authoriser, covered uses and return-to-human conditions. It does not give AI ownership of a gate, investment or material-risk decision. Incidents or worsening results can require less automation.

3.4 Connect the product to family and portfolio decisions#

The Product Family Guide helps related products work together. The Portfolio Guide helps choose which work receives resources. A product can connect directly to the portfolio when no shared family decision is needed.

Send the relevant owner the result, sources, limits, actual costs, expected benefits and proposed next step. Record what comes back: allowed scope, conditions, owner and review point. Update the backlog and checks. Permission for a limited trial does not establish wider benefit or authorise release or scale.

Use Framework Artefacts for how the shared records connect, and Framework Glossary for the common terms. Share the same source records instead of maintaining separate versions for each decision level.

4 Sources#

Further reading

Atlassian’s user-story guide shows how short, non-technical descriptions explain who needs something and why. Atlassian Agile introduces learning through small steps and feedback. These are reading aids, not required templates or tools.

The LeSS framework and Nexus Guide inform attention to the whole product and connections across teams. AIPF’s three decision levels do not prescribe their team structures or events.

The original structure drew on the Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland. The evidence-slice approach also draws on direct and second-hand observation of major Australian Government IT projects with substantial cost and delivery difficulties.
