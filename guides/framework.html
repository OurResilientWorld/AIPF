Purpose

Make better use of what AI makes possible

AIPF helps product owners and organisations manage the rapidly increasing capacity AI enables, so more of that capacity delivers useful results.

AI rapidly increases the capacity to explore ideas, analyse information and produce work. Human capacity to judge usefulness, check evidence, absorb change and support the result does not grow at the same rate. This gap is the core problem AIPF addresses. Product owners and organisations must direct the extra capacity towards useful work and keep its demands within what people can check, use and support.

Why use the guides?

The gap between production and human capacity creates a growing load of suggestions, reviews, unfinished work and changes to absorb. Producing more work without managing that load consumes attention needed to make the work useful. AIPF helps product owners and organisations choose what deserves effort, limit the work in progress and direct AI’s growing capacity towards better products, better services and the outcomes that matter.

The guides help you answer three practical questions:

  • What is worth doing? Choose work that addresses a real need and fits your priorities.
  • How much should we do next? Start with enough work to learn something useful, within a time and cost you can afford.
  • Can people use and support the result? Include checking, adoption, maintenance and the work created for others.

An uncertain piece of work is called a bet: something you think is worth trying, with an owner, agreed time and cost limits, and a point at which you decide whether to continue. That might be research, a small product change or a trial of a new service. A suggestion from an AI agent becomes an active bet only when someone agrees to take it on.

AIPF applies when AI helps create the work, even if the finished product contains no AI. Use the guides alongside the ways you already plan, build and run things.

How the Framework works as a learning loop

Purpose identifies the improvement you want. Principles guide the choices you make, and practices turn those choices into work. Artefacts keep the evidence visible so people can decide what happens next. The return arrow matters: use what you learn to reconsider the purpose, improve the approach or choose a different next step. The principles apply throughout; the boxes show relationships, not separate approval stages.

Different responsibilities, the same aim

All the guides help people make better use of AI’s growing capacity, but they address different responsibilities. A product owner asks whether a product helps its users. A family owner asks whether related products work together. Portfolio decision-makers ask which work deserves the organisation’s money, people and attention. The table below helps you choose the guide for the responsibility you hold and the question in front of you.

For example, when improving customer service, the product decision concerns whether an AI summary helps a staff member answer a request. The family decision concerns how intake, knowledge and response products connect. The portfolio decision concerns whether to invest in this improvement ahead of other work. Each uses the results of the same work to answer a different practical question.

The two product guides adapt the same ideas to different audiences. Choose the approach that fits your situation; they are alternatives at the product level, not successive stages. A product can connect directly to the portfolio when no product-family decision is needed.

Across the guides, useful work begins with a clear need, is checked through a small test and is reconsidered when results arrive. People retain responsibility for choosing and accepting the work. The detail changes with the responsibility: product testing, shared-product coordination or investment across the organisation. Use the relevant guide’s instructions and link the findings so each person can understand the reasons for the next step.

Principles

Keep extra capacity focused on work that helps people

Use these principles to make good choices as AI expands what you can do. They help you spend less effort on work that does not help, spot problems earlier and keep people able to use the results.

Judgement: choose work that matters

Start with the people who need help and the improvement you want to make. Compare the options, including improving something that already exists. AI can suggest many possibilities; people decide which deserve their time and money.

Evidence: check before investing more

Look for observations and test results that show whether the idea works. Include results that challenge it. A convincing explanation or attractive demonstration can help people understand an idea, but it does not show that the idea will work in practice. Checking early helps avoid expensive mistakes.

Restraint: start only what you can properly finish

Agree how much time and money to spend on the next step. Limit the amount of work active at once, including work waiting for review. Leave room for testing, training and support. This keeps growing AI output from overwhelming the people who must make it useful.

Accountability: make someone responsible for the result

Name the person who owns the work and the people who can approve spending or use. Give AI agents clear instructions about what they may do and when they must ask for help. People need to know who will resolve a problem and look after the result after delivery.

Adaptation: use what you learn

Change the work when results, costs or user needs change. Continue what helps; revise, postpone or stop what does not. Keep the learning so people and agents can avoid repeating the same mistakes and put their capacity to better use.

Practices

Use growing capacity without overwhelming people

Use these practices to learn sooner, reduce wasted work and make improvements people can use and support. Apply them to a single product, a group of related products or a portfolio of investments.

Choose a useful next step

State who needs help, what should improve and who owns the work. Identify the most important unknown and the smallest test that would help answer it. Agree the time, cost, people and conditions for stopping. Check whether reviewers and support staff have room for the work before starting it.

Make the idea understandable, then try it

Use a sketch, example or simulation so people can understand the proposed experience. Then test a small connected path through the real work, including what happens when something goes wrong. Make clear which parts are real and which are simulated.

For example, an AI summary may look convincing on screen. A useful test checks whether a staff member can use it to handle a real request, whether important facts are missing and how an error is corrected.

Check the result and the effort it creates

Compare what happened with what you expected. Include user feedback, failed cases and the effort needed to check, integrate, adopt and support the result. Test important AI claims against sources or checks that can reveal mistakes. Keep the agent’s permissions and stopping rules clear.

Record enough for someone else to understand what was tried, what happened and what remains unknown. The Artefacts tab shows how the guides organise that information without requiring a separate document for every item.

Decide what happens next

Compare the next options: continue, change the approach, wait or stop. Say who decides, what work or spending is allowed, what conditions apply and when you will review again. Approval to run a test does not automatically permit a live release or wider use.

Review the results after people start using the product. Revisit the decision when benefits fall short, costs rise, an incident occurs or the AI’s behaviour changes. If work stops, tell those affected, handle continuing service needs and keep what was learned.

Follow the route that fits your audience

Product Owners AI Guide: experience the idea in a simulation, test the smallest real path through the work—a steel thread—then decide whether to fund an MVP, pilot or further test. An approved MVP is built after that investment decision.

Agile Product Guide: explore the need, test a small connected part and deliver through Agile increments. An MVP is normally built after the second decision point and assessed for ongoing use at the third.

Both approaches help people use AI capacity wisely. Follow the chosen guide’s instructions and state what the next decision actually permits, rather than relying on a phase number alone.

Artefacts

Keep the information people need as more work gets done

These records help people find the important facts, avoid repeated explanations and connect work across teams. An artefact is simply something you can inspect: a brief, map, working example, test result or recorded decision. Use the smallest set that helps people understand the work and act on it.

Portfolio choices set the direction and resources. Product-family records connect related products where needed. Either product guide supplies the product’s purpose, test results and decisions. Findings and costs inform the wider choices; priorities and spending limits guide the next work. A product can go directly to the portfolio when no family decision is needed.

Glossary

Use shared meanings to reduce confusion and repeated work

As AI enables more work and more handoffs, clear language helps people and agents understand what is expected. Use familiar words in everyday work. Use this glossary when a guide’s term needs a precise meaning.

The main terms

These terms describe the capacity problem and the information used to manage it. Read them as practical working language: who owns a bet, what time and money it has been given, and what has actually been checked. Use the same meanings in instructions to people and AI agents so a proposal is not mistaken for approved work.

Term Plain-English meaning
AIPF — AI Product Flow Guides to help product owners and organisations manage the rapidly increasing capacity AI enables and use it for worthwhile outcomes.
AI agent An AI system that carries out assigned work, sometimes using tools and files, within agreed permissions.
AI product work Work in which AI helps shape analysis, decisions or delivery. The finished product does not have to contain AI.
Artefact Something people can inspect, such as a brief, map, working example, test result or decision record.
Bet Work you think is worth trying, with an owner, time and cost limits, and a point at which you decide whether to continue.
Capacity How much work people, agents and systems can do. AI increases production capacity faster than human capacity to judge, check, adopt and support the work. Managing that gap is central to AIPF.
Commitment The time, money or activity someone has agreed to provide. State the actual amount and conditions.
Evidence Observations, results or sourced information that someone can check, with enough context to understand their limits.
Assumption Something believed to be true that still needs checking in the situation at hand.
Value hypothesis An explanation of who should benefit and how, which a test can support or challenge.
Accountability Being responsible for a decision or result and for dealing with its consequences.
Decision capacity The time and expertise people have to check work and decide what should happen next.
Work in progress — WIP Work already started but not finished, including work waiting for review. A WIP limit sets how much can be active at once.

Products, tests and learning

These terms help distinguish an idea from a working test and a product people can use. They also explain how work connects across products. Use them to state what has been demonstrated and what still needs checking, especially when someone else will rely on a result or reuse it in a different setting.

Term Plain-English meaning
Product Something intended to help people, such as a service, staff tool, workflow or software product.
Product Owner The person responsible for the product’s purpose, priorities and acceptance, involving specialists and respecting others’ spending or release authority.
Product family Related products that need shared decisions about their combined outcome, dependencies or common foundations. Several teams on one product do not automatically form a family.
Portfolio Work considered together when deciding where to put money, people and attention and how to manage risks across it.
Persona A short description of a representative user’s needs and context. Label assumed characteristics until supported by evidence.
User Story Map User activities shown in order to help choose a small complete journey to build or test.
Value Stream Map A view of the work, handoffs and delays in an existing process.
HTML simulation An interactive example of the proposed experience, normally using mock data and simulated actions. It helps people understand an idea; it is not a working product.
Steel Thread The smallest real path through a product or process that can test an important assumption, including what happens when it fails.
End-to-End Evidence Slice The Agile Product Guide’s narrow connected test, with results, failures and limits. It serves a similar purpose to the Product Owners guide’s steel thread.
MVP — Minimum Viable Product A minimal usable product for learning from real use, with suitable reliability and support. Follow the chosen product guide for when its build is authorised.
Pilot A limited real-world trial with clear participants, responsibilities, learning goals and conditions.
Acceptance criteria Observable conditions used to check whether the result behaves as expected.
Independent checking A check capable of finding mistakes the producer missed. Another AI agreeing with the first is not automatically an independent check.
Learning What results tell you to change or keep in the product, assumptions, tests or way of working.
Pattern A reusable approach, with evidence about where it helps, where it does not and who looks after it.
Shared substrate A common foundation used by products, such as data, a model, infrastructure or a service.
Transfer evidence A check that something which worked in one product or setting also works in another.

Decisions, permissions and follow-up

These terms describe what people may do after a review and who remains responsible. A completed test, a decision to spend and permission to release are different things. Use the table to make the next action clear, then record the actual work, limits, conditions and owner rather than relying on a label or gate number alone.

Term Plain-English meaning
Phase A period of work organised around a question to answer. The two product guides adapt their phases to different audiences.
Gate A point at which someone decides what happens next. Record the allowed work and conditions; a gate number alone does not explain permission.
Proceed / Revise / Defer / Stop Continue with the agreed next step; change the work; wait for a stated condition; or end the authorised work and handle what remains.
Limit / Split Family choices to restrict shared use or separate products’ approaches when one shared solution does not fit.
Merge / Rebalance Portfolio choices to combine related bets or move resources between them.
Scale Extend use or investment when results and support justify it. The portfolio procedure allows this decision at Gate 3.
Acceptance A person with the right responsibility decides that the result meets the stated need or supports the next step, within recorded limits.
Release Permission to make funds available, start work or put something into use. Specify which permission is meant and check its conditions.
AI Model Harness Instructions, permissions, tests and review rules that help keep AI work within agreed limits.
Autonomy boundary The line between what an agent may do on its own and what needs human involvement.
Handoff Passing work or information to someone else with its purpose, owner, results, limits and expected next action.
Source of truth The agreed original record for a fact. Link to it so people do not maintain conflicting copies.
Benefits realisation Checking whether the intended improvement actually happened, for whom and at what cost.
Concentration risk Exposure created when several pieces of work depend on the same model, vendor or other shared component.
Stop condition A result or event that requires work to pause or end, such as a failed test, cost limit or safety problem.
